# Reference — Perkins, *Die with Zero*

Perkins supplies the **objective function**. The other three books help you grow, protect,
and finance wealth; Perkins insists on asking *what the wealth is for.* His answer: to
maximize **lifetime fulfillment**, not terminal net worth. Money is fuel for experiences,
and both your health and your capacity to enjoy money decline with age.

## 1. The nine rules

1. **Maximize your positive life experiences.**
2. **Start investing in experiences early** (they compound — see the memory dividend).
3. **Aim to die with zero** — unspent money is wasted life energy.
4. **Use all available tools to help you die with zero** (annuities, drawdown strategies).
5. **Give money to children/charity when it has the most impact** — while you're alive and
   they most need it, not at death by default.
6. **Don't live your life on autopilot.**
7. **Think of your life as distinct seasons** ("time buckets") — some experiences are
   age-locked and cannot be deferred.
8. **Know when to stop growing your wealth** (the net-worth peak).
9. **Take your biggest risks when you have little to lose** (asymmetric upside, usually
   when young).

## 2. The memory dividend

Experiences are an *investment*: they pay an ongoing "memory dividend" for the rest of
your life. Investing in experiences early lets those dividends compound — the strongest
argument for not deferring all enjoyment to a retirement you may not fully be able to use.

## 3. The personal interest rate (the utility discount rate)

The corollary to declining health: your ability to extract enjoyment from money **falls
with age**, so the effective discount rate on future spending — your **personal interest
rate — rises with age.** At 80 it may exceed 50% or even 100%. This is the concept that
lets Perkins be reconciled with *Principles of Corporate Finance*: he is simply applying a
**personal, age-rising discount rate** to *utility* rather than a market rate to *cash*.
An age-locked experience therefore carries a utility premium that a pure cash-NPV misses.

## 4. The net-worth peak and the survival threshold

- Because the goal is lifetime fulfillment (and, ultimately, zero), net worth should
  **peak and then decline** — it should not climb indefinitely. The peak is a *point in
  time*, set by health and utility, not a number to maximize.
- The floor beneath which you must not fall — the bare-minimum nest egg:

```
Survival threshold = 0.7 × (cost to live one year) × (years left to live)
```

Home equity (via downsizing or a reverse mortgage) can count toward this threshold. Above
the threshold you may safely begin decumulating and weighting Perkins' rules heavily;
below it, you are in Leimberg/Anderson territory (protect and build first).

## When it applies vs. does not

- **Applies:** once the foundation is secure (Leimberg) and the client is at or above the
  survival threshold with genuine surplus; decisions about experience timing, gift timing,
  retirement timing, and whether to keep accumulating.
- **Does NOT apply (or is overridden):** when the client is liquidity-constrained, below
  the survival threshold, or lacks catastrophic-risk protection. Perkins *assumes a safety
  net exists.* Do not invoke "die with zero" to justify draining reserves needed for a
  near-term goal or for foundational security.
