# Reference — Cross-Book Synthesis, Conflict Resolution & Worked Examples

## Where the four frameworks agree, complement, and conflict

**Agreements / complements**
- **Anderson ↔ Corporate Finance are mathematically the same claim.** Anderson's "paying
  down debt earns a return equal to its after-tax cost" *is* the NPV/opportunity-cost rule
  applied to a liability. Use Corporate Finance to *compute* the spread Anderson tells you
  to look for.
- **Leimberg ↔ everyone.** Leimberg's cash-flow-timing lens and foundation-first ordering
  frame *when* each of the others is allowed to speak. Nobody optimizes leverage or spends
  down toward zero until the emergency reserve and catastrophic-risk cover exist.
- **Perkins ↔ Corporate Finance.** Perkins' "personal interest rate" is just an
  age-rising discount rate applied to *utility*. Same discounting machinery, different
  numerator (fulfillment, not cash).

**Conflicts**
- **Anderson (hold liquidity, use leverage, keep building) vs. Perkins (stop
  over-accumulating, spend down, die with zero).** Anderson optimizes the balance sheet;
  Perkins optimizes the life. They collide once the client has *surplus*.
- **Pure NPV ("borrow cheap, invest the spread") vs. Perkins ("money later is worth much
  less to you").** A thin positive financial spread can be the wrong call if the cash
  would fund an age-locked experience now.

## The resolution principle (who wins, and when)

Priority is **conditional on where the client sits**, not fixed:

1. **Below the foundation line** (no 3–6 month reserve, or uninsured catastrophic risk):
   **Leimberg wins outright.** Secure the base. The only optimization allowed is killing
   oppressive debt.
2. **Foundation secure, still accumulating, has cheap/flexible debt:** **Anderson +
   Corporate Finance drive.** Classify debt, compute after-tax spreads, respect the
   glide-path D/A ratio and liquidity guardrails. Perkins is a minor tilt at most.
3. **Foundation secure AND at/above the survival threshold with genuine surplus:**
   **Perkins' weighting rises sharply.** Now age-locked experiences get a utility premium,
   the net-worth-peak question is live, and "invest the spread forever" needs a reason to
   beat "use it while it matters."
4. **Any liquidity-constrained multi-goal case:** the **liquidity stress test is a hard
   gate** that sits above the NPV ranking — a higher-NPV plan that can strand a committed
   near-term goal loses to a lower-NPV plan that preserves optionality.

## Worked example — funding a graduate degree while preserving funds for a second one

*Query shape:* "I have $X liquid. I want to pay for a master's now and possibly a second
degree in ~3 years. Should I pay tuition in cash, take the student loan, or something
else?"

**How the Financial Planner reasons:**

1. **Goals & timelines (Leimberg 1–2):** Goal A = degree now; Goal B = degree in ~3 yrs
   (probability p, cost estimate). Both are *cash-flow-timing* goals. Clarify amounts,
   the loan rate and tax treatment, the tax bracket, expected return on liquid savings,
   risk tolerance, and how firm Goal B is.
2. **Foundation gate (Leimberg 3):** Confirm 3–6 months' reserve and catastrophic-risk
   cover exist *outside* the $X. If not, that comes first.
3. **Classify the tuition financing (Anderson):** A subsidized/low-cost student loan at,
   say, 5–7% is **working debt**, not oppressive (oppressive = > inflation + 6%). It is a
   candidate to *keep*, not reflexively avoid.
4. **Compute the spread (Corporate Finance):** Compare the loan's **after-tax cost**
   `rD(1 − Tc)` against the after-tax return on keeping $X liquid/invested. If the loan is
   6% with no deduction and safe liquid return is ~4–5%, the *financial* cost of borrowing
   is a small negative spread — but you are buying **optionality for Goal B**.
5. **Liquidity stress test (the hard gate):** If paying Goal A entirely in cash leaves
   ~$0 for Goal B, then a modest, deferred-payment student loan for Goal A that *preserves
   the cash pool* is usually superior even at a slightly negative financial spread —
   because it keeps Goal B fundable. This is Anderson's "value liquidity / sh\*t happens"
   meeting Leimberg's cash-flow-timing. Do **not** drain to zero for one goal.
6. **Glide-path / guardrail (Anderson):** Check that total resulting D/A stays within the
   band for the client's net-worth-to-income multiple; don't over-lever.
7. **Perkins tilt:** Usually minor here (education is an accumulating investment, not an
   age-locked experience) — but if Goal B is speculative and the surplus is large, don't
   let debt-aversion or over-accumulation crowd out present-life value.
8. **Recommendation + sensitivity:** e.g., *"Take the low-cost loan for Goal A, keep $X
   liquid earmarked for Goal B. This trades a ~1–1.5% annual spread for the ability to
   fund both goals. This flips if: (a) the loan rate exceeds ~8% [becomes oppressive —
   then pay cash], (b) Goal B's probability drops near zero [then pay Goal A in cash and
   save the spread], or (c) your liquid return exceeds the loan rate [then definitely
   borrow]."*

## Worked example — pay off a low-rate mortgage vs. invest a windfall

- **Classify:** 3.5% mortgage (after-tax ~2.7% if deductible) = **enriching/working
  debt**, not oppressive.
- **Spread (Corporate Finance):** after-tax cost ~2.7% vs. risk-appropriate expected
  return on investing; a diversified portfolio's expected after-tax return typically
  exceeds 2.7%, so NPV favors *investing / keeping the debt*.
- **Anderson:** paying it off earns a guaranteed ~2.7% — below his ~4% "don't accelerate"
  threshold — and destroys liquidity. Keep the mortgage; keep the windfall liquid/invested.
- **Leimberg:** ensure reserve/insurance intact first.
- **Perkins:** if the client is past the survival threshold with large surplus and the
  mortgage payoff would buy peace-of-mind that unlocks living better *now*, the utility
  case can override a thin financial spread — surface it as an explicit, quantified
  trade, not a default.
