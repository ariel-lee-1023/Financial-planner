# Reference — Leimberg et al., *The Tools & Techniques of Financial Planning*

This book supplies the **process wrapper and the foundation-first gatekeeper**. It does
not tell you the optimal leverage or spending level — it tells you the *order* in which
decisions must be made and enforces the discipline of securing the base before
optimizing the top.

## 1. Financial planning is, at root, cash-flow planning

> "In the purest sense, financial planning is, quite simply, cash flow planning. It is
> planning to have available the amount of cash needed at the time it is needed."

Every recommendation exists to serve a **cash-flow goal** — the right amount of cash, at
the right time, in the right place. This reframes multi-goal problems (e.g., two degrees)
as a **cash-flow timing problem**: what must be liquid, and when, for each goal to be met
without derailing the others.

## 2. The seven-step planning process

Follow this sequence; do not skip to recommendations before the situation and goals are
understood.

1. **Understand** the client's personal and financial circumstances.
2. **Identify and select** goals (and their timelines and priorities).
3. **Analyze** the client's current course of action and potential alternatives.
4. **Develop** the recommendation(s).
5. **Present** the recommendation(s).
6. **Implement** the recommendation(s).
7. **Monitor** progress and **update**.

For an AI answering a one-shot query, steps 1–2 map to *asking clarifying questions*
before advising; steps 3–5 map to *the analysis and the recommendation*; steps 6–7 map
to *the implementation note and the "what would change this" sensitivity flag*.

## 3. The hierarchy of financial needs and risks (foundation first)

A Maslow-style ordering: lower needs must be satisfied before higher ones.

- **Emergency reserve** — a long-standing rule of thumb: **three to six months of income
  (or essential expenses)** held in liquid assets (money-market funds, savings). This is
  the "first level" / firm foundation on which everything else is built.
- **Protection against catastrophic risk (the insurance principle)** — insure against any
  loss "large enough to place other wealth-accumulation objectives in jeopardy"
  (disability, death of an earner, liability, major health event). You do not optimize a
  portfolio or a leverage strategy while a single uninsured event could wipe it out.
- **Only then** — accumulation, leverage optimization, and lifestyle/experience spending.

## When it applies vs. does not

- **Applies:** always, as the outer loop. Especially decisive in multi-goal sequencing
  and any time the base (reserve + catastrophic-risk cover) may not be in place.
- **Does NOT do:** it is process, not prescription. It won't produce the optimal
  numbers; it ensures you solve the right problem in the right order and never optimize a
  structure that a foreseeable shock could destroy.
